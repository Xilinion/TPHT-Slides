  \note{Build this slide in two beats. First the pale bars: this is the most data each table can hold, the last insert before it resizes or fails --- a third of memory for chaining, seventy and seventy-five percent for cuckoo and linear probing. Then the second beat, which is the honest part: nobody runs a table there, because filling it that far costs throughput. From half load to the peak, cuckoo loses forty-one percent of its insert throughput, nine million operations per second down to five point three, and linear probing loses twenty-one percent, eighteen point six down to fourteen point six. So the space you actually get is the solid bar, not the dashed line. And that is the whole motivation: you cannot fill your way out of the overhead, you need a table that is not paying it in the first place.

Numbers and their provenance, if anyone asks. All of it comes from the same load sweep as the speed--space slide later in the talk, where we fill each table in five percent load steps until it resizes or fails; the half-load figures are that same curve read at half the peak load. The implementations are TBB for chaining, libcuckoo for cuckoo, Junction for linear probing.

Two honest caveats to have ready. First, chaining is the exception to the throughput claim: it actually gains about seven percent going from half load to peak, which is why its row carries no percentage. The reason is that it allocates a node per item, so filling it does not crowd anything --- but it is also six times slower than the others in absolute terms, three million operations per second against eighteen, so it never had throughput to protect. Second, chaining is also why its two marks are close together: its footprint shrinks with the item count, so its space efficiency barely moves with load, whereas the fixed-array tables lose exactly half.

Motivation. Hash tables are among the most used data structures and have been studied for seven decades, so it is fair to ask what is left to do. The answer is memory: even at its very best a deployed table hands you a third to three quarters of the memory it holds, and in practice well under that. The rest goes to pointers, head arrays, empty slots kept for speed, metadata and fingerprints, and the full key written down again even though the table already knows where it hashed.}

  \note{This slide introduces Flattened-TPHT in a simplified way for the audience. We do not need to exactly follow every construction detail from the paper. The story is: each key bucket owns a cache-line home block. We append quotient-compressed tuples into the home block first. If we cannot append a full tuple, we store a tiny pointer to one tuple in the overflow table. Stress that this is not a linked list: the overflow tuple does not iteratively point to more overflow tuples. If more room is needed in the home block, we can push one existing tuple to the overflow table and replace it by a tiny pointer, which creates more space for new home-resident tuples or more tiny pointers. This works because quotienting shaved bytes off the stored keys. If the audience asks whether four home slots, two pointer slots, and the overflow table are enough, say that the paper analyzes these capacity questions and sizes the overflow pool using the spill tail; the slide gives the key numbers and the measured result that queries average about 1.25 cache misses.}

        best measured \keyterm{speed--space} trade-off\par}
  \end{columns}
  \note{This is the paper's conclusion in one slide. Lead with the thesis: tiny pointers and key quotienting are two ideas that lived in the theory literature, and the contribution is engineering them into hash tables people can actually ship. Then the two designs and what each one is for. Chained-TPHT is, to our knowledge, the first practical succinct hash table --- it uses less space than the data itself while keeping constant-time operations, which is the part a theory audience will care about. Flattened-TPHT is the one to reach for if latency matters: the cache-aware layout keeps the common case to a single memory access, and it is still more space-efficient than every baseline we compared against. On the last line, spell out what ready for real systems means: both variants support deletions, they resize online without any stop-the-world pause, and they integrate cleanly with plain 64-bit keys and values. That is what makes this usable rather than a paper artifact. Numbers are on the previous slides if anyone asks.}
